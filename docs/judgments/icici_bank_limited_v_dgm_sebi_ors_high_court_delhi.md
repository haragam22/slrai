## BORROWER'S CLAIM

The banks, including ICICI Bank, HDFC Bank, Kotak Mahindra Bank, Bajaj Finance, and others, alleged that the Reserve Bank of India (RBI), as the Central Public Information Officer (CPIO), intended to disclose their Inspection Reports and Risk Assessment Reports (RARs) in response to an RTI application filed by Shri Girish Mittal, without adequately considering their objections. They contended that such disclosure would violate the confidentiality of sensitive commercial and client data shared with the RBI under statutory obligation and in fiduciary capacity. They argued that the information was exempt from disclosure under Section 8(1)(a), (d), (e), (h), and (j) of the RTI Act, 2005, as it included trade secrets, personal information of customers, and data whose release would prejudice the banks' competitive position and economic security. They further claimed that the CPIO and First Appellate Authority (FAA) violated the principles of natural justice by failing to grant them a personal hearing and by issuing non-speaking, mechanically identical orders that did not address their specific objections. The banks prayed that the FAA's order be set aside and that the disclosure be stayed, particularly given that a related writ petition (WP(C) No. 1159/2019) was pending before the Supreme Court.

## HOLDING SUMMARY

The Central Information Commission (CIC) held that while the functional relationship between the RBI and banks is not fiduciary, the information contained within Inspection and Risk Assessment Reports often includes data that banks hold in a fiduciary capacity from their clients, such as personal financial details and transaction histories. The Commission emphasized that the right to information under the RTI Act must be balanced against the fundamental right to privacy and the protection of commercially sensitive information. It ruled that the CPIO and FAA acted arbitrarily by issuing cryptic, non-speaking orders without providing a reasoned analysis of the banks' objections or granting them a personal hearing, thereby violating the principles of natural justice. The Commission set aside the orders of the CPIO and FAA and remanded the matter for a fresh adjudication. It directed that the CPIO must conduct a detailed, case-by-case analysis, applying the public interest test, and must consider redacting sensitive portions of the reports rather than making a blanket disclosure or non-disclosure. This applies when: a public authority intends to disclose regulatory reports containing third-party confidential or personal information without a reasoned order or hearing, and a related constitutional challenge is pending before a higher court.

## KEY FACTS OF THIS CASE

The RTI applicant, Shri Girish Mittal, filed an application on 01.05.2021 seeking Inspection Reports and Risk Assessment Reports (RARs) of 13 major banks and NBFCs, including ICICI Bank, for the preceding three years. The RBI, acting as the CPIO, issued notices to these financial institutions under Section 11 of the RTI Act, indicating its intent to disclose the reports. The banks filed objections, arguing that the reports contained highly sensitive commercial data, trade secrets, and personal information of their clients, the disclosure of which would harm their competitive position and violate customer privacy. The FAA dismissed their first appeals with brief, identical orders. The banks then filed second appeals before the CIC. The case arose in the context of the Supreme Court's earlier judgment in *RBI v. Jayantilal N. M. Mistry*, which had held that the RBI is not in a fiduciary relationship with banks, but had not fully addressed the confidentiality of client data within the reports. A related writ petition (WP(C) No. 1159/2019), filed by several banks challenging the disclosure, was pending before the Supreme Court.

## WHAT THE COURT DECIDED

The CIC set aside the orders of the CPIO and the FAA for being non-speaking and for violating the principles of natural justice by not providing a personal hearing or a reasoned analysis of the banks' objections. The Commission remanded the matter back to the CPIO for a fresh decision. It directed the CPIO to conduct a detailed, case-by-case examination of the reports, apply the public interest test, consider the applicability of exemptions under Section 8 of the RTI Act, and decide on a redaction-based approach. The Commission also noted that the pendency of a related writ petition in the Supreme Court warranted caution, suggesting that the CPIO could await the outcome of that case to avoid causing irreparable harm.

## KEY QUOTE

The Commission is of the view that the protection of disclosure of information under Section 8(1) of the RTI Act, 2005 in the hands of financial institutions does not evaporate once such data/information is shared, in good faith and trust, with the regulator under statutory obligation.

## CONDITION: WHEN THIS JUDGMENT APPLIES

This judgment applies when:
1. A public authority (like RBI) intends to disclose regulatory reports (e.g., Inspection Reports, RARs) in response to an RTI application.
2. The reports contain information that a third party (e.g., a bank) holds in a fiduciary capacity from its clients (e.g., personal financial data).
3. The third party has filed specific objections to the disclosure based on confidentiality, commercial sensitivity, or privacy.
4. The public authority has issued a non-speaking order without a reasoned analysis of the objections.
5. The public authority has not granted a personal hearing to the third party.
6. A related constitutional challenge to the disclosure is pending before a higher court.

## CONDITION: WHEN THIS JUDGMENT DOES NOT APPLY

None.

## STATUTORY CONTEXT

Primary law: Right to Information Act, 2005
Primary provision: Section 8(1) — "(1) Notwithstanding anything contained in this Act, there shall be no obligation to give any citizen, information which relates to... (d) trade or commercial secrets, including intellectual property, the disclosure of which would harm the competitive position of a third party; (e) information available to a person in his fiduciary relationship, unless the competent authority is satisfied that larger public interest warrants the disclosure of such information; (j) information which relates to personal information the disclosure of which has no relationship to any public activity or interest, or which would cause unwarranted invasion of the privacy of the individual..."
Instrument level: ACT
Nature of provision: MANDATORY — the exemptions are mandatory, but the public interest test allows for disclosure if it outweighs the harm.

Secondary: Section 11(1) RTI Act — "Where a public authority intends to disclose information which relates to information available to a person in his fiduciary relationship, it shall give a written notice to the third party... and take into consideration any representation made by that third party."
Nature: MANDATORY — the notice and opportunity to be heard are mandatory.

## RELATIONSHIP TO OTHER JUDGMENTS

Follows: RBI v. Jayantilal N. M. Mistry (2016) 3 SCC 525
  Affirmed that the RBI is not in a fiduciary relationship with banks in its regulatory capacity, but clarified that this does not negate the fiduciary relationship between the bank and its clients whose data is contained in the reports.

Follows: Central Board of Secondary Education v. Aditya Bandopadhyay (2011) 8 SCC 497
  Applied the definition of "fiduciary relationship" to the banker-customer context, recognizing that customer data is held in trust.

Follows: K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017) 10 SCC 1
  Emphasized the need to balance the right to information with the fundamental right to privacy.

Follows: Central Public Information Officer, Supreme Court of India v. Subhash Chandra Agarwal (2020) 5 SCC 481
  Reinforced the requirement of a "public interest test" when qualified exemptions under Section 8(1) are involved.
